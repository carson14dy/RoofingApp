About this site

A plain weather page for roofing work in Montana. It gathers public data from the National Weather Service and NOAA every 10 minutes and shows it with its time and source.

What each part is

  • Warnings and advisories are the National Weather Service's official alerts for Montana, shown word for word. Alerts issued by county or zone have no outline on the map but are still listed.
  • The map plays the past hour of NOAA's radar estimate (MRMS, every 10 minutes) and then the HRRR computer model's forecast: every 15 minutes for the next hour, then hourly to 12 hours. Before "Now" it is radar; after "Now" it is a model forecast, and the map always says which. Far from a radar site the beam passes over low clouds, so a blank area is not proof of dry weather; areas with no radar coverage are hatched. The temperature and wind-gust layers are the same model. The numbers on the towns are the NWS forecast.
  • Town forecasts are NWS forecasts for the 2.5 km grid square around each town. They are predictions for an area, not measurements at a roof.
  • Storm reports are preliminary hail and wind reports gathered by the Storm Prediction Center. Hail sizes are as reported; a report is a point, not the edge of a storm.
    • {% if storm_layers %}
  • Radar echo tops are NOAA's radar estimate (MRMS) for each town: the highest sampled height above ground with 35 dBZ or more, among heights that had radar data, in metres above sea level ("at least" when heights above it had no radar value). The 11 heights run from 500 to 15,000 m above sea level. They are not observed hail or damage, and no echo is not an all-clear.
    • {% endif %} {% if storm_layers %}
  • Satellite picture is NOAA's GOES infrared picture (band 13, 10.3 µm) of the newest 5-minute scan over the map's area, in kelvin. It is an observation of the infrared brightness temperature of the cloud tops and of the ground where there is no cloud. It is not a height and not a hail signal. GOES-East is read first; GOES-West is used when GOES-East fails or has no scan in the last hour. An uncorrected cloud top is drawn away from the satellite (north-west for GOES-East, north-east for GOES-West), about 3 to 4 km for a 2 km top and 20 to 33 km for a 12 to 15 km top. Missing pixels are hatched, never drawn as clear sky. Areas warmer than 300 K are left uncoloured, and missing areas show hatch lines; uncoloured is not a statement about the weather.
    • {% endif %}

Hail history

The hail history is NOAA's radar estimate (MRMS MESH) of the largest hail over this cell, about 0.7 miles north to south and 0.5 miles east to west, during the day, for the five years to the newest day in the store. The cell is the one under the spot you tap or click on the map, and each town's page has the history for the cell at its own spot. A day runs from 12:00 UTC to 12:00 UTC the next day: 6 am to 6 am Mountain daylight time, 5 am to 5 am in winter (the SPC reporting day). Sizes are shown to the hundredth of an inch, rounded down, with the millimetre value exact; a day below 0.25 in (6.35 mm) is not stored. A day with no radar estimate is not the same as no hail: radar misses some hail, and a day with no file is listed and not counted. An estimate is not a measurement at the ground and not an inspection. Days not listed have no radar estimate of 0.25 in or more; that is not the same as no hail. The newest day is added by a job that starts at 12:43 UTC and shows here on the next rebuild, usually within the hour. The as-of date is on every page that shows the history. The units and the source are in the data sources table below.

Other records for the same cell

The history also joins the other kinds of record for the same cell. Each row keeps its own kind word, and no record is turned into another kind: reports are what people sent the National Weather Service (NWS local storm reports within 5 miles, of every type); official records are NOAA's Storm Events database (hail and thunderstorm wind points within 5 miles, and the zone-coded rows of every type, such as high wind, winter storm and drought, for the cell's public forecast zone); radar detections are the NEXRAD hail signatures within 2 miles, the radar algorithm's detection for a storm cell and not hail on the ground; official warnings are the NWS severe thunderstorm, tornado and flash flood polygons that covered the cell, with the forecast tags they carried at issue time; zone products are the NWS high wind, winter storm, blizzard, ice storm, extreme cold, wind chill, dust storm, wind advisory and flood watch products for the cell's whole public forecast zone, named, and they cover the whole zone, not this spot alone; red flag warnings are the NWS fire weather zone products for the cell's fire weather zone, a separate zone map from the public forecast zones, and they cover that whole fire weather zone too; and station readings are the peak gusts at the two nearest airport stations within 40 miles, which are at the airport and not at this address.

Not joined: the SPC storm reports (they come from the same reports as the local storm reports, so they would count twice); the HRRR modelled gust (a model, not a reading); and the DAT storm survey points (few in Montana). Not joined yet: NOAA's county-coded Storm Events records (points, not zone rows) of tornado, flood, flash flood, heavy rain, funnel cloud, lightning and debris flow; the same types appear among the reports when people sent them to the NWS. The flood warnings, flood watches and flood advisories as polygons, the areal flood warnings and areal flood advisories, the county-only areal flood watches, and the flash flood watches; snow squall warnings; and the winter weather advisories, winter storm watches, heat advisories, excessive heat warnings and watches, frost advisories and freeze warnings. Other NWS products are not joined yet. Known gaps: NCEI's database runs behind the other sources, and the sheet says to which date; SWDI's inventory is not complete; the wind unit of the local storm reports is inferred; and warning tags are the forecast threat at issue time, not what fell.

Base map imagery

The map's base is a white map of public outlines. In the Layers menu you can switch it to aerial imagery with roads and names from USGS, The National Map. USGS says its map services are free and in the public domain. Its topographic maps have some exceptions: USGS says most US Topo maps from 2010 to 2016 contain commercially licensed road data, and it has not stated a licence for this service's road and name layers. The credit on the map reads "Imagery: USDA, USGS, The National Map". The imagery is off unless you switch it on or open a link that switches it on. A link that opens on Imagery loads the tiles as the page opens. Your IP address and the map area you view go to USGS while it is on. The white map makes no request to any other site.

USGS asks that products derived from its map services carry this acknowledgment: "Map services and data available from U.S. Geological Survey, National Geospatial Program." (USGS FAQ: terms of use and licensing for map services)

Flags

An hour is flagged for gusts of {{ limits.gust_mph }} mph or more, sustained wind of {{ limits.wind_mph }} mph or more, a {{ limits.pop_pct }}% or higher chance of rain or snow, or a temperature under {{ limits.cold_f }} °F or over {{ limits.hot_f }} °F. These are placeholders until the company sets limits for its roof systems. A missing value never raises a flag and never clears one. An hour without a flag is not a promise that work is safe.

When something fails

If a source doesn't answer, the page says so and shows a dash instead of a number. If the alert list can't be refreshed, the last list that was checked stays on the page with its time. The line under the header lists each source and whether it loaded. If a page is more than 25 minutes old, it says so.
